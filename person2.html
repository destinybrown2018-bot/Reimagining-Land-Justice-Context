Booker T. Washington (1856–1915)

Washington was an influential African American educator, author, and leader in the post-Civil War United States. Born into slavery, he rose to prominence as the founder of Tuskegee Institute in Alabama, a school focused on vocational and agricultural education for Black Americans.

Washington believed that economic self-reliance, land ownership, and practical skills were key to improving the lives of formerly enslaved people. He urged Black Americans to build strong communities through hard work and education, often promoting cooperation with white leaders to gradually gain respect and rights—a philosophy sometimes called the "Atlanta Compromise."

While Washington's approach was pragmatic and focused on immediate improvements in the lives of Black Americans, it was also controversial. Critics, including W.E.B. Du Bois, argued that his emphasis on vocational training and accommodation to segregation did not sufficiently challenge systemic racism or advocate for civil rights.
